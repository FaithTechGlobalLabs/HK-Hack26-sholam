# Shalom: 132 Discovery Questions and Teaching Notes

**Audience:** Young people, approximately ages 13–20.  
**Purpose:** A content foundation for Shalom lessons. Each entry gives a direct answer, a primary Scripture passage, supporting passages, explanation in context, and a takeaway.

## Editorial standards

- Answer the exact question in the first paragraph.
- Explain what the cited passage says and why it matters.
- Be confident about well-established teachings while identifying genuine disagreements and unknown details.
- Do not use a Bible verse as proof of a scientific or historical claim it does not address.
- Treat disturbing passages seriously and do not turn them into jokes or easy moral slogans.
- Read the full passage in context and have disputed or sensitive lessons reviewed before publication.

**Count:** 132 questions: 100 core Discover questions and 32 in “Wait, That’s in the Bible?”

## Everyday Life, Social Media & Identity

### FM001 | Why do we care so much about what other people think of us?
*Medium · Real Life, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Galatians 1:10
**Supporting Scripture:** John 12:42–43, Proverbs 29:25

**Clear answer**

We care because people are social. Being accepted helps us feel safe and connected, so criticism or exclusion can hurt. The problem begins when approval matters more than honesty, conscience, or the people we care about.

**Why this passage matters**

In Galatians 1:10, Paul asks whether he is trying to please people or serve Christ. He is not saying that feedback is useless. He is asking whose approval gets the final say when doing what is right may cost popularity. John 12:42–43 gives a concrete example: some leaders believed in Jesus but stayed quiet because they feared losing status. The Bible understands the pull of approval and warns against letting it control our decisions.

**Key takeaway**

Listen to people you trust, but do not let popularity decide your values.

### FM002 | Is it possible to be confident without becoming arrogant?
*Medium · Real Life, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Philippians 2:3–8
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 18:9–14, Romans 12:3

**Clear answer**

Yes. Confidence means recognizing your abilities and worth; arrogance means treating those abilities as proof that you are above other people. You can take pride in doing something well and still remain humble.

**Why this passage matters**

Philippians 2 points to Jesus as the model: although he had status, he chose the position of a servant. Paul is not asking Christians to pretend they have no gifts. Romans 12:3 says to think of yourself with honest judgment, neither exaggerating nor denying what you have been given. The Pharisee in Luke 18 shows the opposite problem: he builds his identity by looking down on someone else.

**Key takeaway**

Know what you are good at without using it to look down on others.

### FM003 | Why does social media make everyone else's life look better than ours?
*Medium · Real Life, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Galatians 6:4–5
**Supporting Scripture:** Philippians 4:11–13, Psalm 139:13–16

**Clear answer**

Because social media gives us a stream of other people's selected moments, while we experience our own lives in full. You see their holiday, achievement, or best photo, but not the stress, ordinary days, arguments, or disappointments around it.

**Why this passage matters**

Galatians 6:4–5 tells people to examine their own work instead of constantly measuring themselves against someone else. Paul is addressing pride and responsibility, but the principle also exposes how comparison can distort our view of life. A post can be genuine and still be incomplete. Philippians 4 describes learning contentment in both plenty and need, not pretending every situation feels good. The issue is not that other people have good things; it is assuming their highlights prove your life is worse.

**Key takeaway**

A feed shows moments, not the whole life behind them.

### FM004 | If a joke humiliates someone, is it still just a joke?
*Medium · Real Life, Challenging · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Ephesians 4:29
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 12:36, Proverbs 26:18–19

**Clear answer**

No. Calling something a joke does not make it harmless. If the humour depends on embarrassing, targeting, or demeaning someone, it can still be cruel even when other people laugh.

**Why this passage matters**

Ephesians 4:29 asks Christians to use speech that helps rather than damages the people listening. That does not mean humour is wrong or that friends can never tease each other. It means the relationship, the target, and the effect matter. Proverbs 26:18–19 compares someone who hurts a neighbour and then says 'I was only joking' to a person who throws dangerous weapons and denies responsibility. A joke can be funny and still require an apology.

**Key takeaway**

The test is not only whether people laughed, but whether someone was needlessly hurt.

### FM005 | Can you forgive someone and still not trust them?
*Deep · Real Life, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 17:3–4
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 18:15–17, Romans 12:18

**Clear answer**

Yes. Forgiveness means giving up revenge and refusing to let resentment control you. Trust means believing someone is likely to act safely and honestly. Trust usually has to be rebuilt through consistent behaviour.

**Why this passage matters**

In Luke 17:3–4, Jesus connects forgiveness with naming wrongdoing and repentance. He does not tell people to pretend harm never happened. Matthew 18:15–17 also describes confronting a problem and seeking accountability. Reconciliation can be a good goal, but it may take time, and it is not always safe or possible. If someone continues to manipulate, threaten, or abuse you, keeping distance and getting help can be the right choice.

**Key takeaway**

You can forgive without giving someone immediate access to hurt you again.

### FM006 | Is it wrong to enjoy attention or want to be famous?
*Medium · Real Life, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Matthew 6:1–6
**Supporting Scripture:** Mark 10:42–45, John 12:43

**Clear answer**

No. Wanting recognition, enjoying praise, or hoping to become well known is not automatically selfish. It becomes a problem when attention becomes the main reason you act or when you compromise your values to keep it.

**Why this passage matters**

In Matthew 6:1–6, Jesus criticizes people who give, pray, or fast mainly so others will admire them. His target is performative goodness, not every public achievement. Mark 10:42–45 adds that leadership should serve rather than use people for status. Wanting your work to matter can be healthy. Needing constant applause can make your choices depend on an audience instead of what is good.

**Key takeaway**

Enjoy recognition, but do not let it become the purpose of everything you do.

### FM007 | When does ambition turn into obsession?
*Medium · Real Life, Challenging · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 8:34–36
**Supporting Scripture:** Philippians 3:12–14, 1 Timothy 6:9–10

**Clear answer**

Ambition becomes obsession when the goal starts controlling your identity and damaging everything around it. Warning signs include sacrificing your health, relationships, honesty, or basic responsibilities because you cannot tolerate slowing down or failing.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 8:34–36, Jesus asks what someone gains by winning the whole world but losing their life. He is not condemning hard work or achievement. He is challenging the idea that success is worth any price. Paul pursues his calling with discipline in Philippians 3, but his purpose is larger than status. A goal should give direction to your life, not consume the life it was supposed to improve.

**Key takeaway**

A worthwhile goal should not require you to lose yourself to reach it.

### FM008 | Would Jesus cancel someone for something they said years ago?
*Medium · What If, Real Life · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 19:1–10
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 7:1–5, Matthew 18:15–17, Luke 17:3–4

**Clear answer**

Jesus would take the wrongdoing seriously, but he would not reduce a person to their worst post or treat public humiliation as justice. In the Gospels, he confronts sin while also calling people to change and make things right.

**Why this passage matters**

Luke 19 shows Jesus welcoming Zacchaeus, a tax collector who had exploited people. That welcome does not excuse the harm: Zacchaeus responds by repaying those he cheated. Matthew 7:1–5 warns against condemning others while ignoring our own failures, and Matthew 18:15–17 describes direct accountability. Applied to an old online post, the questions would include what was said, whom it harmed, whether the person understands it, and whether their actions have changed. Forgiveness does not mean there are never consequences.

**Key takeaway**

Hold people accountable while leaving room for truth, repair, and genuine change.

### FM009 | Does being kind mean you have to be nice to everyone?
*Medium · Real Life, Challenging · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Romans 12:9–18
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 23:23–28, Galatians 6:1

**Clear answer**

No. Kindness is not the same as agreeing with everyone or allowing people to mistreat you. You can speak honestly, disagree, say no, and set boundaries while still treating someone with dignity.

**Why this passage matters**

Romans 12:9–18 describes love as sincere: it rejects evil, holds on to good, and seeks peace where possible. Jesus also speaks sharply to religious leaders when he sees hypocrisy and exploitation. His honesty is not permission for cruelty; it is a refusal to call harmful behaviour good. If someone is unsafe, leaving or asking for help can be wiser than trying to keep the peace at any cost.

**Key takeaway**

Be respectful toward people without approving everything they do.

### FM010 | Can you be friends with someone whose values are completely different from yours?
*Medium · Real Life, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** John 4:7–26
**Supporting Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 15:33, Romans 12:18

**Clear answer**

Yes. You can care about someone, listen to them, and build a real friendship without sharing all their beliefs. Friendship does not require agreement, but it does require respect and honesty.

**Why this passage matters**

In John 4, Jesus speaks with a Samaritan woman despite deep religious, ethnic, and social divisions. He does not avoid the difficult differences between them, but he treats her as a person worth speaking with and takes her questions seriously. The passage is not saying beliefs never matter. It shows that disagreement is not a reason to dehumanize or automatically avoid someone. You can keep your convictions while being genuinely interested in another person's life.

**Key takeaway**

You do not have to agree with someone to treat them as a neighbour.

### FM011 | Why is apologizing so difficult, even when you know you're wrong?
*Medium · Real Life, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Matthew 5:23–24
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 15:11–32, James 5:16

**Clear answer**

Apologizing is hard because it means giving up your defence, admitting that someone was hurt, and accepting that you cannot control how they respond. Pride often makes us focus more on protecting our image than repairing the relationship.

**Why this passage matters**

In Matthew 5:23–24, Jesus says that if you remember a conflict with someone, you should seek reconciliation before presenting your gift at the altar. The point is that worship and relationships cannot be neatly separated. A real apology names what you did, avoids excuses, and tries to repair the damage. It does not demand instant forgiveness from the other person.

**Key takeaway**

A sincere apology takes responsibility instead of trying to win the argument.

### FM012 | Is it hypocritical to struggle with something you believe is wrong?
*Deep · Real Life, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Romans 7:15–25
**Supporting Scripture:** 1 John 1:8–9, Galatians 5:16–17

**Clear answer**

No. Struggling to live by a belief does not automatically make you a hypocrite. Hypocrisy is pretending to be better than you are, hiding wrongdoing while judging others, or refusing to admit the gap between your values and your actions.

**Why this passage matters**

Romans 7 describes the frustration of wanting to do what is right and still doing what is wrong. Christians differ on the precise interpretation of Paul's experience, but the passage clearly recognizes the struggle between intention and action. The New Testament does not present growth as instant perfection. It calls people to be honest, repent, seek help, and keep changing. The danger is not admitting a struggle; it is using a moral standard to condemn others while refusing to face yourself.

**Key takeaway**

Honest struggle is different from pretending you have nothing to work on.

### FM013 | Would Jesus use social media if He were alive today?
*Medium · What If, Real Life · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 1:35–39
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 5:13–16, Ephesians 4:29

**Clear answer**

We cannot know whether Jesus would use a modern platform, because the Gospels say nothing about social media. But his teaching gives a clear way to judge how a person uses one: does it serve truth, love, and others, or feed pride, cruelty, and constant attention-seeking?

**Why this passage matters**

Mark 1:35–39 shows Jesus stepping away from growing public demand to pray and then choosing where to go next. He did not let popularity decide his entire schedule. Matthew 12:36 also treats words as morally important. A platform could spread teaching and encourage people, but it could also reward outrage and performance. The useful question is not whether Jesus would have an account, but what he would do with the attention and influence.

**Key takeaway**

The platform is a tool; the purpose and behaviour matter more.

### FM014 | Is it ever right to stay silent when someone is being treated unfairly?
*Medium · Real Life, Challenging · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Proverbs 31:8–9
**Supporting Scripture:** Esther 4:12–16, James 4:17

**Clear answer**

Yes. If someone is being harmed and you can help safely, silence can allow the unfairness to continue. Speaking up does not always mean confronting the person directly; it can mean supporting the target, documenting what happened, or telling someone with authority.

**Why this passage matters**

Proverbs 31:8–9 calls people to speak for those whose voices are being ignored and to judge fairly. The instruction is about using influence on behalf of people who may not be able to defend themselves. Wisdom still matters: if speaking publicly would make the danger worse, find a safer way to help. Esther's story also shows a person weighing risk before using her position to protect others.

**Key takeaway**

Do not confuse staying out of drama with staying silent while someone is harmed.

### FM015 | Can someone do the right thing for the wrong reason?
*Medium · Curious, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Matthew 6:1–4
**Supporting Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 13:1–3, 1 Samuel 16:7

**Clear answer**

Yes. You can do something helpful while being motivated by pride, guilt, fear, or a desire for praise. The action may still benefit someone, but the motive shapes what the action means and whether it will continue when nobody is watching.

**Why this passage matters**

In Matthew 6:1–4, Jesus describes giving to people in need and warns against doing it to attract admiration. The recipient may still receive real help, but the giver is treating generosity as a performance. This does not mean motives are always pure or that you should stop helping until they are. It means you should examine why you act and learn to care about the person, not just the reaction you receive.

**Key takeaway**

Do good because someone needs help, not only because people might notice.

## Jesus: The Unexpected Details

### FM016 | Why did Jesus wash His disciples' feet?
*Easy · Surprising, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** John 13:1–17
**Supporting Scripture:** Mark 10:42–45, Philippians 2:5–8

**Clear answer**

Jesus washed his disciples' feet to show that real leadership serves instead of demanding to be served. He took the work of a household servant and did it for his own followers.

**Why this passage matters**

In John 13, foot-washing is an act of hospitality normally done by a servant because people walked dusty roads in sandals. Peter is uncomfortable because he thinks the teacher should not take the lowest position. Jesus turns that expectation around: if he, their Lord and teacher, serves them, they should serve one another. The detail that Judas is present makes the scene even more striking. Jesus knows betrayal is coming and still kneels to serve.

**Key takeaway**

In Jesus' view, greatness is shown by serving people, not standing above them.

### FM017 | Why did Jesus spend so much time with people everyone else avoided?
*Easy · Jesus, Curious · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 5:27–32
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 19:1–10, Mark 2:15–17

**Clear answer**

Jesus spent time with outsiders because he came to seek people who were excluded, judged, or aware of their need for help. He did not treat someone's reputation as a reason they were beyond God's attention.

**Why this passage matters**

In Luke 5, Jesus calls Levi, a tax collector, and then eats with tax collectors and others whom religious leaders disapprove of. Tax collectors worked within a system associated with Roman rule and could profit by collecting more than required, so many people viewed them with suspicion. Jesus' meal does not mean dishonesty is acceptable. It shows that he approaches people in order to call them to change, not to keep them at a distance until they become respectable.

**Key takeaway**

Jesus does not wait for people to look respectable before offering them a way back.

### FM018 | Why did Jesus tell people not to talk about some of His miracles?
*Medium · Curious, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 1:40–45
**Supporting Scripture:** Mark 8:27–30, Mark 5:18–20

**Clear answer**

Jesus sometimes told people to keep a miracle quiet because public excitement could distort his mission. Crowds might focus on getting another healing or treat him as the kind of political deliverer they expected, while missing his teaching and the meaning of his identity.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 1:40–45, Jesus heals a man with a skin disease and tells him to follow the required priestly procedure rather than spread the story. The man publicizes it anyway, and Jesus can no longer enter towns openly because the crowds gather. Mark repeatedly shows people misunderstanding Jesus by focusing on miracles alone. The instructions are often called the 'Messianic secret', though the reasons vary across episodes.

**Key takeaway**

A miracle mattered, but Jesus did not want his whole message reduced to spectacle.

### FM019 | Why did Jesus flip tables in the temple?
*Easy · Surprising, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 11:15–18
**Supporting Scripture:** Isaiah 56:7, Jeremiah 7:9–11

**Clear answer**

Jesus overturned tables because he was condemning the way the temple had become a place of exploitation and exclusion instead of a house of prayer. It was a deliberate prophetic protest, not a random burst of anger.

**Why this passage matters**

Mark 11 places the temple action between two parts of the fig-tree story, linking them as signs of fruitlessness. People needed animals and currency for worship, so the presence of sellers and money changers was not automatically wrong. Jesus' words about a 'den of robbers' echo the prophets' warning against religious activity that hides injustice. He is challenging a system that claims to honour God while failing to serve people and welcome prayer.

**Key takeaway**

Religious activity means little when it is separated from justice and genuine worship.

### FM020 | Why did Jesus ask questions when He already knew the answers?
*Medium · Curious, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 10:51
**Supporting Scripture:** John 5:6–9, Genesis 3:9

**Clear answer**

Jesus asked questions to make people explain what they wanted, reveal what they believed, and think more deeply. A question can help someone take part in an answer instead of simply receiving information.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 10:51, Jesus asks the blind Bartimaeus, 'What do you want me to do for you?' His need seems obvious, but the question lets Bartimaeus speak for himself and name what he is asking. Jesus also uses questions to expose assumptions, as when he asks his disciples who they say he is. His questions are not signs that he lacks knowledge; they are part of how he teaches and relates to people.

**Key takeaway**

Sometimes the right question helps a person understand what they truly need.

### FM021 | Did Jesus have brothers and sisters?
*Easy · Curious, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 6:3
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 13:55–56, Galatians 1:19

**Clear answer**

The Gospels name Jesus' brothers and mention his sisters, so the New Testament clearly says he had siblings or close family members described that way. Christians disagree about whether the brothers and sisters were Mary's other biological children, step-siblings, or relatives.

**Why this passage matters**

Mark 6:3 names James, Joses, Judas, and Simon as Jesus' brothers and mentions his sisters. Matthew 13:55–56 gives a similar list. Many Protestants understand them as younger children of Mary and Joseph. Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions generally hold that Mary remained ever-virgin, so they interpret these family terms differently. The text itself does not explain the precise family relationship, which is why the debate continues.

**Key takeaway**

The New Testament mentions Jesus' siblings, but Christians interpret the exact relationship differently.

### FM022 | What happened to Jesus between the ages of 12 and 30?
*Easy · Mystery, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 2:41–52
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 3:23, Mark 6:3

**Clear answer**

The Bible gives almost no detailed account of Jesus' life from age 12 until he began his public ministry, probably around age 30. It says he returned to Nazareth, lived within his family, and grew in wisdom and maturity.

**Why this passage matters**

Luke 2:41–52 tells the story of Jesus at the temple when he is 12. After that, Luke summarizes his life in one sentence: he was obedient to his parents and grew in wisdom, stature, and favour with God and people. Mark later identifies him as a carpenter or craftsman (Mark 6:3). Stories claiming to describe his childhood in detail appear in later writings, but they are not part of the New Testament and cannot be treated as reliable biography.

**Key takeaway**

Most of Jesus' early life is not recorded; the Gospels focus on his public ministry.

### FM023 | Why did Jesus choose fishermen and a tax collector as disciples?
*Easy · Surprising, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 3:13–19
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 4:18–22, Matthew 9:9–13

**Clear answer**

Jesus chose an unlikely group because his movement was not built around the most powerful or prestigious people. Fishermen, a tax collector, and others with very different backgrounds became the people who learned from him and carried his message.

**Why this passage matters**

Mark 3:13–19 says Jesus chose twelve disciples to be with him and to be sent out to preach. Fishermen did skilled, physically demanding work; Matthew or Levi worked in tax collection, a job many Jews associated with Roman control and exploitation. The Gospels also show the disciples misunderstanding Jesus and competing for status. Their selection is not a claim that they were naturally perfect. It shows that Jesus formed a community through teaching, calling, and patience.

**Key takeaway**

Jesus built his group around people willing to follow and learn, not people with perfect reputations.

### FM024 | Why did Jesus sometimes heal people with a touch and other times just speak?
*Medium · Curious, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 1:40–42
**Supporting Scripture:** John 4:46–54, Mark 7:31–37

**Clear answer**

The Gospels show Jesus healing in different ways because the method was not the source of the power. Sometimes he touched someone, sometimes he spoke, and sometimes he used a physical action. The result depended on his authority, not a fixed technique.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 1:40–42, Jesus touches a man with a skin disease, something that made many people wary because of ritual impurity and social exclusion. In other stories he heals at a distance with a word. John 9 includes mud and washing. The variety keeps readers from treating healing like a formula or believing that a particular gesture guarantees a miracle. The stories focus on Jesus' compassion and authority.

**Key takeaway**

Jesus' healing was not a technique people could copy to control God.

### FM025 | What did Jesus mean when He said the last would be first?
*Medium · Curious, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 10:42–45
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 20:1–16, Luke 14:7–11

**Clear answer**

Jesus meant that God's idea of greatness is almost the opposite of the world's ranking system. People who seem unimportant now may be honoured by God, while people who chase status may discover that status counts for little.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 10:42–45, James and John ask for positions of honour. Jesus contrasts rulers who use power over others with his own example: he came to serve and give his life. 'The last will be first' is not a promise that every unsuccessful person will become famous later. It overturns the assumption that wealth, rank, and influence prove someone's value. In God's kingdom, humble service matters more than being at the top.

**Key takeaway**

God measures greatness by faithfulness and service, not social rank.

### FM026 | Why did Jesus cry if He knew He was going to raise Lazarus?
*Medium · Curious, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** John 11:32–36
**Supporting Scripture:** John 11:1–44, Luke 19:41–44

**Clear answer**

Jesus cried because he genuinely loved Lazarus and the people grieving him. Knowing he would raise Lazarus did not make their pain unreal or stop him from feeling sorrow with them.

**Why this passage matters**

John 11 says Jesus was deeply moved when he saw Mary and the others weeping. The shortest verse, 'Jesus wept', comes before he calls Lazarus out of the tomb. The story holds grief and hope together: Jesus does not tell the family to stop crying because a miracle is coming. He enters their mourning first. It also shows that Christian hope in resurrection does not require pretending death and loss do not hurt.

**Key takeaway**

Hope does not cancel grief; Jesus shows that love can weep with people.

### FM027 | Did Jesus ever get frustrated with His disciples?
*Easy · Curious, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 8:14–21
**Supporting Scripture:** Mark 9:19–29, Mark 6:30–44

**Clear answer**

Yes. The Gospels show Jesus becoming frustrated when his disciples repeatedly miss the point, act out of fear, or fail to understand what he is teaching. His frustration is directed at their lack of understanding, not at their worth as people.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 8:14–21, the disciples worry that they have only one loaf of bread. Jesus asks a series of questions about the miracles they have already witnessed, trying to help them understand why he is not talking about literal bread. They have seen him feed thousands, yet they still think narrowly. The scene is honest about how slow learning can be. Jesus corrects them, but he continues teaching and travelling with them.

**Key takeaway**

Even people close to Jesus needed repeated correction and time to understand.

### FM028 | Why did Jesus spend 40 days in the wilderness?
*Easy · Surprising, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Matthew 4:1–11
**Supporting Scripture:** Deuteronomy 8:2–3, Mark 1:12–13

**Clear answer**

Jesus spent 40 days in the wilderness being tested before beginning his public ministry. The temptations challenged what kind of Messiah he would be: one who uses power for himself, demands spectacular proof, or takes authority through worshipping evil.

**Why this passage matters**

Matthew 4:1–11 deliberately echoes Israel's 40 years in the wilderness. Jesus answers each temptation with words from Deuteronomy, where Israel's own wilderness failures are remembered. He refuses to turn stones into bread for himself, force God to rescue him, or gain the kingdoms of the world by compromise. The episode shows that Jesus' mission will follow trust and obedience rather than shortcuts to popularity or power.

**Key takeaway**

Jesus begins his mission by refusing to gain power through selfishness or compromise.

### FM029 | What made people so angry when Jesus forgave sins?
*Medium · Curious, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 2:1–12
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 7:36–50, Isaiah 43:25

**Clear answer**

People were angry because Jesus claimed to forgive a man's sins, something they believed belonged to God. When Jesus then healed the man, he presented the healing as evidence that he had authority to forgive.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 2:1–12, friends lower a paralyzed man through a roof because the crowd blocks the entrance. Jesus first says his sins are forgiven. Some scribes silently question this because, in their view, only God can forgive sins. Jesus asks which is easier to say and heals the man so they can see his authority. The story is not saying the man's disability was caused by sin. It connects healing with Jesus' deeper claim about who he is.

**Key takeaway**

The argument was about Jesus' authority to forgive, not about blaming the man's illness on sin.

## Strange & Surprising Bible Stories

### FM030 | Why did a donkey start talking in the Bible?
*Easy · Surprising, Bible Story · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Numbers 22:21–35
**Supporting Scripture:** 2 Peter 2:15–16, Jude 11

**Clear answer**

In Numbers 22, God makes Balaam's donkey speak to stop Balaam from walking blindly into danger. Balaam is on his way to curse Israel for a king who wants to pay him, and the donkey sees the angel of the Lord blocking the road before Balaam does.

**Why this passage matters**

The donkey turns aside, crushes Balaam's foot against a wall, and finally lies down. Balaam beats it each time, until God opens the animal's mouth and it asks why it is being struck. Then Balaam's eyes are opened and he sees the angel with a drawn sword. The humour is deliberate: the supposedly insightful prophet cannot see what his animal sees. The story exposes greed and spiritual blindness, not a normal ability of animals to speak.

**Key takeaway**

Balaam is rebuked because the animal sees God's warning before the prophet does.

### FM031 | What happened when a man fell asleep during Paul's very long sermon?
*Easy · Funny, Bible Story · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Acts 20:7–12
**Supporting Scripture:** Acts 20:13–16

**Clear answer**

Eutychus fell asleep while Paul was speaking late into the night, fell from a third-floor window, and was picked up dead. Paul went down, embraced him, and the young man was found alive. The story presents his recovery as a miracle.

**Why this passage matters**

Acts 20 takes place during a late-night gathering on Paul's final visit to Troas. Many lamps are burning, and Paul continues teaching because he plans to leave the next day. Eutychus, sitting in an upstairs window, falls into a deep sleep and falls three stories. Paul tells the people not to panic because his life is in him. Afterward they return upstairs, share a meal, and are greatly comforted. The point is not that long sermons are dangerous; it is a story of a community shaken and then comforted.

**Key takeaway**

The passage describes an accident, Paul's response, and a remarkable restoration of life.

### FM032 | Could Jonah really have survived inside a giant fish?
*Medium · Curious, Bible Story · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Jonah 1:17–2:10
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 12:38–41, Jonah 3:1–10

**Clear answer**

Jonah 1:17 says God provided a 'great fish' that swallowed Jonah, and Jonah survived there for three days and nights. The text does not identify the animal as a whale or explain a natural process that could keep a person alive inside it. The story presents this as a miracle.

**Why this passage matters**

Jonah's prayer in chapter 2 describes him as near death, surrounded by waters, and then rescued. The central issue in the book is not marine biology; it is Jonah's refusal to take God's message to Nineveh and his anger when God shows the city mercy. Jesus later refers to Jonah's three days in the fish as a sign connected to his own death and resurrection (Matthew 12:39–41). Christians differ over how to understand the book's genre and historical details, but the text's purpose is clear: God's mercy reaches even people Jonah despises.

**Key takeaway**

The fish is part of the miracle; the book's deeper challenge is whether we want mercy for our enemies too.

### FM033 | Why did God tell Hosea to marry someone who would be unfaithful?
*Medium · Surprising, Bible Story · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Hosea 1:2–3; 3:1–5
**Supporting Scripture:** Hosea 2:14–23, Jeremiah 3:1–10

**Clear answer**

Hosea's marriage became a living picture of Israel's unfaithfulness to God. The relationship was used as a prophetic sign: Israel had turned to other gods, while God continued to call the people back. The book does not present betrayal as harmless or make this a model for marriage.

**Why this passage matters**

Hosea 1–3 links the prophet's family life with Israel's covenant relationship with God. The names of the children and the marriage imagery communicate judgment and the possibility of restoration. The language is deliberately painful because the book wants readers to feel the seriousness of Israel's betrayal. Interpreters debate details about Gomer and the events behind the account. Whatever view is taken, the passage should not be used to tell someone to remain in an abusive relationship; it is an acted prophetic message, not a general instruction for spouses.

**Key takeaway**

Hosea's marriage symbolized Israel's betrayal and God's call to return, not a rule for accepting abuse.

### FM034 | Why did Jacob wrestle with a mysterious stranger all night?

**Supporting Scripture:** Hosea 12:3–5, Genesis 35:9–12

**Clear answer**

Jacob wrestled through the night because he was facing a mysterious divine encounter just before meeting Esau, the brother he had cheated and feared. By dawn he had received a blessing and a new name, Israel, but he also left with a permanent injury.

**Why this passage matters**

Genesis 32 calls the stranger a 'man', yet Jacob says he has seen God face to face. Hosea 12 later describes the encounter in terms of an angel. The text leaves some mystery about the figure's identity, but it makes the result clear: Jacob is changed. He can no longer rely on clever schemes and physical strength in the same way. His limp becomes a lasting reminder of the night he struggled for blessing.

Jacob leaves with a blessing, a new name, and a reminder that he is not in control.

**Supporting Scripture:** 1 Samuel 16:7–13, Psalm 20:7

David defeated Goliath by using a sling, a weapon he knew how to handle, rather than wearing Saul's armour and fighting the giant on his terms. The story presents the victory as an act of trust in God, not proof that skill and preparation do not matter.

In 1 Samuel 17, Goliath is an experienced heavily armed warrior who intimidates Israel's army. David rejects Saul's armour because he has not trained in it and uses the sling he knows from protecting his sheep. A sling was a real ancient weapon capable of launching a stone with lethal force. David's speech makes the story's theological point: the battle is not decided by size or equipment but by the God Israel has forgotten to trust.

David wins by using the skills he has and trusting God rather than copying Goliath.

**Clear answer**

God spoke to Moses through a bush that burned without being consumed to get his attention and call him to lead the Israelites out of slavery in Egypt. The sign showed that this was not an ordinary fire and introduced Moses to the God who had heard his people's suffering.

In Exodus 3, Moses sees the bush while tending sheep near Horeb. God tells him to remove his sandals because the ground is holy, then identifies himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The burning bush marks God's call to Moses and his promise to rescue Israel.

*Easy · Surprising, Bible Story · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Joshua 6:1–20
**Supporting Scripture:** Joshua 5:13–15, Hebrews 11:30

**Clear answer**

Joshua 6 says Jericho's walls fell after Israel marched around the city, blew trumpets, and shouted at God's command. The story presents the victory as God's, not as a military trick that the Israelites discovered.

**Why this passage matters**

The Israelites are told to circle the city for seven days, carrying the ark and blowing trumpets. On the seventh day they march seven times, shout, and the wall collapses. The ritual makes no ordinary military sense; it teaches Israel that the land is not being won by superior force alone. Archaeologists debate how this account relates to the physical history and dating of Jericho, so the biblical story should not be presented as settled archaeological proof of a particular battle.

The story's point is that Israel's victory is attributed to God's action, not its military strength.

**Supporting Scripture:** Mark 14:3–9, Matthew 26:6–13

**Clear answer**

Mary poured expensive perfume on Jesus' feet to honour him with an extravagant act of love. Jesus connects her action with his approaching burial, so the gift becomes a sign that she understands the seriousness of what is about to happen.

**Why this passage matters**

John 12 places the meal in Bethany shortly before Jesus enters Jerusalem. Mary uses costly perfume made from pure nard and wipes his feet with her hair. Judas objects that the perfume could have been sold and the money given to the poor, but John says he was not genuinely concerned for them. Jesus tells Judas to leave Mary alone and connects the act with the day of his burial. This is not the same scene as every other Gospel's anointing story, and Mary should not be confused with the unnamed woman in Luke 7.

Mary's costly gift shows devotion to Jesus just before his death.

**Clear answer**

Peter starts sinking because he becomes frightened by the wind and waves after stepping out of the boat toward Jesus. He calls for help, and Jesus immediately reaches out and catches him.

Matthew 14 describes the disciples struggling in a storm when they see Jesus walking on the water. Peter asks to come to him, steps out, and begins walking. When he notices the wind, fear takes over. Jesus asks why he doubted, but he does not leave Peter to drown. The scene is not saying that strong faith makes a person immune to fear or danger. It shows Peter's trust, his limits, and Jesus' willingness to rescue him.

Peter's fear is real, but Jesus reaches for him before he sinks.

**Supporting Scripture:** Judges 6:11–16, 1 Corinthians 1:27–29

**Clear answer**

God reduced Gideon's army from 32,000 to 300 so Israel could not claim that its own strength had won the battle. The victory was meant to show that God, not a large army, was responsible for delivering Israel from Midian.

**Why this passage matters**

In Judges 7, God says the army is too large because Israel might boast, 'My own hand has saved me.' The selection process leaves Gideon with only 300 men. They carry trumpets, jars, and torches rather than normal battle equipment. The Midianite camp panics and turns against itself. This is a specific story about Israel's deliverance, not a general strategy that says smaller armies always win.

The small army makes the source of the victory unmistakable in the story.

**Supporting Scripture:** Proverbs 16:18, Daniel 5:18–23

**Clear answer**

Daniel 4 says King Nebuchadnezzar was brought low after becoming proud and taking credit for his power and achievements. He lost his human status for a period, lived like an animal, and later acknowledged that God rules over human kingdoms.

**Why this passage matters**

The king boasts about the great Babylon he has built, and Daniel's warning comes true. The description of hair like eagle feathers and nails like bird claws is vivid, but the text does not give enough medical information to diagnose a modern mental illness. The story's focus is his humiliation and recovery. He is restored when he recognizes that his authority is not absolute and that human pride cannot make a person sovereign.

Nebuchadnezzar's story warns that power and success do not make anyone greater than God.

**Supporting Scripture:** Jeremiah 7:8–11, Mark 11:15–18

**Clear answer**

Jesus cursed the fig tree as a symbolic warning about fruitlessness. In Mark's Gospel, the tree surrounds the story of Jesus overturning tables in the temple, so the two events help explain each other: religious activity was visible, but the justice and prayer God wanted were missing.

**Why this passage matters**

Jesus sees leaves on the tree but no fruit, then later the disciples find it withered. Mark places the temple confrontation between those moments. The fig tree is therefore more than a story about hunger or frustration. It acts like a prophetic sign against a religious system that looks alive but fails to produce faithfulness. The timing detail, that it was not the season for figs, makes the action deliberately symbolic rather than ordinary gardening advice.

The tree is a picture of outward religion that produces no real fruit.

**Clear answer**

Exodus 14 says God made a path through the sea so the Israelites could escape Pharaoh's army. After the people crossed, the waters returned and the pursuing Egyptian forces were overwhelmed.

**Why this passage matters**

The Israelites are trapped between the sea and Pharaoh's chariots. Moses tells them to stand firm, and God drives the waters back through a strong wind. The story remembers the event as Israel's defining rescue from slavery. The Hebrew term can be translated 'Sea of Reeds', and scholars debate the precise location and natural setting. The biblical account presents the crossing as God's decisive act of deliverance, not simply a clever escape route.

The sea crossing becomes the Bible's central picture of God rescuing Israel from slavery.

## Life in Jesus' Time

### FM044 | What did people actually eat in Jesus' time?
*Easy · History, Curious · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Matthew 14:13–21
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 24:36–43, John 21:9–13

**Clear answer**

Most people in Jesus' time ate simple foods such as bread, olives, olive oil, lentils, beans, vegetables, fruit, fish, and sometimes meat. What someone ate depended on their wealth, location, season, and whether it was an ordinary day or a feast.

**Why this passage matters**

The Gospels mention bread and fish repeatedly because they were familiar foods in Galilee. Wheat or barley bread, olives, dates, figs, grapes, and wine were common parts of the diet. Fish mattered especially around the Sea of Galilee, while meat was more likely at festivals or special meals because it cost more. Jesus' feeding of the crowds uses ordinary food to tell a story about abundance and God's care, not to provide a complete menu of first-century meals.

**Key takeaway**

Everyday meals were usually simple, local, and shaped by income and season.

### FM045 | What was a normal day like for a teenager in ancient Israel?
*Medium · History, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 2:41–52
**Supporting Scripture:** Deuteronomy 6:6–9, Proverbs 1:8–9

**Clear answer**

A teenager in ancient Israel probably spent much more time helping the household than attending school. Depending on the family, that could mean farm work, caring for animals, fetching water, preparing food, learning a trade, or helping with family business.

**Why this passage matters**

Luke 2 gives one rare glimpse of Jesus at age 12, travelling with his family to Jerusalem for Passover. It does not describe a modern school system or a typical teenage timetable. Most people lived in rural communities, and work began young because households depended on everyone's contribution. Some boys learned a trade from their fathers or relatives; girls were commonly trained in household work, though actual responsibilities varied. We should not pretend every teenager had the same life.

**Key takeaway**

Teenage life was shaped by family work, community, religion, and a much less formal education system.

### FM046 | How did people travel without cars, trains or planes?
*Easy · History, Curious · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 10:30–37
**Supporting Scripture:** Acts 20:13–16, Acts 27:1–6

**Clear answer**

People travelled mainly by walking. Those with money or a reason to transport goods might use donkeys, mules, horses, or carts, while boats were important for crossing lakes and travelling along the coast.

**Why this passage matters**

The Good Samaritan story begins with a man travelling the road from Jerusalem to Jericho, a steep route that descended through rough country. Most ordinary journeys were made on foot, often in groups for safety. Travel could take hours or days depending on distance, terrain, weather, and road conditions. Wealthy people had more options, but there was nothing like modern public transport or private cars for most people.

**Key takeaway**

Walking was the normal way to travel, and a journey could be slow, tiring, and risky.

### FM047 | What did houses look like in the time of Jesus?
*Easy · History, Curious · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 2:1–12
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 5:17–26, Acts 10:9–16

**Clear answer**

Many ordinary homes in Galilee were modest stone or mud-brick buildings with flat roofs, small windows, and rooms arranged around a courtyard. Wealthier homes could be larger and better decorated, so there was no single house design for everyone.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 2, a crowd gathers around a house where Jesus is teaching. Friends carry a paralyzed man onto the roof, open it, and lower him down. Many homes had accessible flat roofs used for drying food, working, or sleeping in warm weather. The Gospel does not provide an architectural plan, but archaeology from villages such as Capernaum helps show the compact, simple homes common in the region. The story's roof detail would have been understandable to its first readers.

**Key takeaway**

Most homes were practical and compact, with flat roofs that were part of everyday life.

### FM048 | Why were tax collectors so hated?
*Easy · History, Jesus · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 19:1–10
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 9:9–13, Luke 5:27–32

**Clear answer**

Tax collectors were hated because they collected money for Roman rule and could exploit the system to make themselves rich. People often saw them as collaborators who profited from foreign occupation and from their neighbours' hardship.

**Why this passage matters**

Rome used local contractors and officials to collect many taxes, and the system could encourage overcharging. The Gospels do not claim every tax collector was equally corrupt, but the job carried a strong stigma. Zacchaeus is described as a chief tax collector and wealthy; after meeting Jesus, he promises to repay people he has cheated (Luke 19:1–10). His response shows why Jesus' decision to eat with tax collectors shocked respectable observers.

**Key takeaway**

The hatred came from the link between tax collection, Roman control, and opportunities for abuse.

### FM049 | What was so shocking about Jesus talking to the Samaritan woman?
*Medium · History, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** John 4:1–42
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 10:25–37, 2 Kings 17:24–34

**Clear answer**

It was shocking because Jesus, a Jewish man and religious teacher, spoke publicly with a Samaritan woman. Jews and Samaritans had a long history of religious and ethnic hostility, and men and women who were not relatives did not normally have this kind of public conversation.

**Why this passage matters**

In John 4, the woman is surprised that Jesus asks her for water. The conversation moves from their communities' disagreement about worship to her questions about the Messiah. Jesus treats her as someone capable of serious theological conversation and reveals his identity to her. The text does not say she was an immoral woman simply because she had had several husbands; readers should not add that assumption. She becomes one of the people who tells her town about Jesus.

**Key takeaway**

Jesus crosses social boundaries and treats the woman as a serious person, not an outsider to avoid.

### FM050 | How did weddings work in Jesus' time?
*Easy · History, Curious · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** John 2:1–11
**Supporting Scripture:** Matthew 25:1–13, John 3:29

**Clear answer**

A Jewish wedding in Jesus' time was a major family and community celebration, often involving a procession, food, music, and a feast. The exact customs varied by place, family, and wealth, but the celebration could continue for several days.

**Why this passage matters**

At Cana in John 2, Jesus attends a wedding where the wine runs out. That would have caused embarrassment for the hosts because hospitality was a serious responsibility. Jesus turns water into wine, and John calls this the first of his signs, revealing his glory to the disciples. The passage is not a detailed manual of wedding customs. It uses a familiar celebration to show Jesus bringing unexpected abundance and beginning to reveal who he is.

**Key takeaway**

Weddings were public celebrations, and running out of food or drink could bring real shame on a family.

### FM051 | What did a Roman crucifixion actually involve?
*Medium · History, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 15:15–39
**Supporting Scripture:** John 19:1–37, Isaiah 52:13–53:12

**Clear answer**

Roman crucifixion was a deliberately brutal public execution designed to shame the condemned person and warn everyone else not to challenge Roman power. It usually involved a beating, public exposure, and being fixed to a cross or stake until death.

**Why this passage matters**

Mark 15 describes Jesus being mocked, stripped, beaten, and crucified under Roman authority. Crucifixion was normally used against enslaved people and non-citizens, making it a degrading punishment as well as a death sentence. The exact procedure could vary, and historians debate details such as how long death took in individual cases. For Christians, the horror matters because they believe Jesus willingly entered the violence and shame of human injustice rather than avoiding it.

**Key takeaway**

Crucifixion was meant to humiliate and terrorize as much as to kill.

### FM052 | Why were shepherds considered outsiders in some parts of society?
*Medium · History, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 2:8–20
**Supporting Scripture:** John 10:1–18, Psalm 23

**Clear answer**

Shepherds were not automatically despised or excluded from society. They did, however, work outdoors for long hours, could be poor, and sometimes had a low social reputation. Luke's story is striking because the first announcement of Jesus' birth goes to ordinary working people, not political or religious elites.

**Why this passage matters**

Luke 2 describes shepherds staying in the fields at night when angels announce Jesus' birth. Later traditions sometimes claim all shepherds were considered dishonest or ritually unclean, but that broad claim goes beyond what Luke says. Shepherding was essential work in an economy built around animals, land, and food. The scene highlights God's message reaching people with little public status.

**Key takeaway**

The shepherds matter because the announcement reaches ordinary people, not just the powerful.

### FM053 | What jobs did ordinary people have in the Roman Empire?
*Easy · History, Curious · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 6:3
**Supporting Scripture:** Acts 18:1–3, Luke 5:1–11

**Clear answer**

Most people in the Roman world worked in farming or related trades. Around Galilee, ordinary jobs included growing grain and olives, fishing, building, carpentry, weaving, pottery, stonework, trading, and transporting goods. Some also served as soldiers, officials, or household workers.

**Why this passage matters**

The Gospels mention fishermen such as Peter and Andrew, a carpenter or craftsman such as Jesus, and tax collectors such as Matthew. These examples reflect a society where many people worked with their hands and lived close to subsistence. Wealth and job opportunities were uneven: a small elite controlled much land and money, while many workers had little financial security. Slavery was also part of the Roman economy, though the experiences of enslaved people varied widely.

Most people earned a living through agriculture, fishing, crafts, trade, or service work.

### FM054 | How did people learn and read before modern schools?
*Medium · History, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 4:16–21
**Supporting Scripture:** Acts 8:26–35, Deuteronomy 6:6–9

**Clear answer**

People learned mainly through family, apprenticeships, religious gatherings, and oral teaching. Some boys received more formal instruction in reading and writing, but modern universal schooling did not exist, and literacy levels varied greatly.

**Why this passage matters**

Luke 4 describes Jesus reading from Isaiah in a synagogue. This shows that Scripture was read aloud and interpreted in local Jewish communities. Children learned prayers, stories, laws, and customs from their families and community; someone preparing for a trade learned by working alongside an experienced person. Written texts were expensive to produce, so hearing and remembering material mattered much more than it does in a world of cheap books and phones.

**Key takeaway**

Learning happened through family, community, religious reading, memory, and hands-on work.

### FM055 | Why was the road between Jerusalem and Jericho so dangerous?
*Easy · History, Bible Story · 2 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 10:25–37
**Supporting Scripture:** 2 Chronicles 28:15, Matthew 20:29

**Clear answer**

The Jerusalem-to-Jericho road was dangerous because it passed through steep, rocky, isolated terrain where travellers could be attacked and help might be far away. The route descended roughly 1,000 metres over about 25 kilometres, making it physically demanding as well.

**Why this passage matters**

Jesus uses this road in the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37). A traveller is robbed, beaten, and left for dead. A priest and a Levite pass by, while a Samaritan stops to help. The setting makes the danger believable, but the main point is not a travel warning. Jesus challenges the idea that neighbour-love is limited to people from your own group.

The dangerous road makes the Samaritan's risk and compassion part of the story.

## Big Questions About God & Faith

### FM056 | If God knows everything, do we actually have free will?
*Deep · Big Question, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Deuteronomy 30:15–20
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 13:34, Proverbs 16:9

**Clear answer**

Christians generally believe human choices are real and morally meaningful, even though God knows everything. Knowing what someone will choose is not the same as forcing them to choose it. How God's foreknowledge fits with human freedom is debated among Christians and philosophers.

**Why this passage matters**

Deuteronomy 30 presents Israel with a genuine choice: life and good, or death and evil. The people are told to choose life. This language assumes responsibility rather than treating their decisions as meaningless. Some Christian traditions explain freedom through God's knowledge of choices without causing them; others emphasize God's sovereignty and understand freedom differently. Both are trying to take seriously the Bible's claims that God knows and rules, while people remain accountable for their actions.

**Key takeaway**

The Bible treats human decisions as real, even while Christians debate how freedom relates to God's foreknowledge.

### FM057 | Why doesn't God stop every terrible thing from happening?
*Deep · Big Question, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Romans 8:18–25
**Supporting Scripture:** Genesis 3:14–19, John 11:32–36, Revelation 21:1–5

**Clear answer**

In Christian teaching, God chose to create a world where people have real freedom and nature follows dependable patterns. Those conditions make relationships, responsibility, and ordinary life possible, but they also mean people can hurt one another and natural events can cause terrible damage. Christians believe God does not ignore suffering: he calls people to fight it and promises to renew creation. The Bible does not explain why he permits every particular tragedy.

**Why this passage matters**

Romans 8 describes the whole creation as groaning under decay, not just people suffering because of other people's choices. That helps explain why the problem includes disease, disaster, and death as well as cruelty. The Christian answer is not that every tragedy has a hidden lesson or that God approves of it. Jesus heals the sick, grieves with mourners, and teaches his followers to care for people in need. Revelation 21 gives the promised end of the story: God will remove death and mourning. Christians still cannot identify why God allows one specific tragedy and prevents another.

**Key takeaway**

Christians believe suffering is real, evil is to be resisted, and God's final purpose is restoration.

### FM058 | If God is good, why does He allow suffering?
*Deep · Big Question, Deep · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Romans 8:18–25
**Supporting Scripture:** Genesis 3:14–19, John 9:1–3, Revelation 21:1–5

**Clear answer**

The central Christian answer is that God created a good world, but human rebellion and the brokenness of creation have brought sin, pain, and death into it. God permits people to make real choices, even when they use that freedom to harm others. Christianity also says God entered human suffering through Jesus and promises to end it, although it cannot explain every individual tragedy.

**Why this passage matters**

Genesis 3 describes human disobedience bringing conflict and disorder into human life. Romans 8:18–25 widens the picture: creation itself is subject to decay and longs for renewal. Free will helps explain violence and betrayal, but it does not fully explain earthquakes, disease, or a child's illness. The Gospels show Jesus grieving, healing, and suffering rather than treating pain as unimportant. Revelation 21:4 gives the final Christian hope: death and mourning will not have the last word.

**Key takeaway**

Christianity does not call suffering good; it explains it as part of a broken world that God intends to renew.

### FM059 | Can science and belief in God both be true?
*Medium · Big Question, Science · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Psalm 19:1–4
**Supporting Scripture:** Romans 1:19–20, Colossians 1:15–17

**Clear answer**

Yes. Science studies the natural world and how it works; belief in God addresses questions such as why anything exists and whether the universe has purpose. They can conflict when someone makes a claim that contradicts evidence, but scientific investigation does not by itself prove or disprove God.

**Why this passage matters**

Psalm 19 describes the heavens as declaring God's glory. It is a poem of worship, not a scientific explanation of stars or the atmosphere. Many Christians see scientific discovery as a way of understanding the order and complexity of creation. Others disagree about how particular scientific findings relate to biblical passages. The important distinction is to avoid treating a religious poem as a laboratory report or using 'science' as if it answers every philosophical question.

**Key takeaway**

Science explains natural processes; theology asks further questions about meaning, purpose, and God.

### FM060 | If God created everything, who created God?
*Medium · Big Question, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Psalm 90:1–2
**Supporting Scripture:** Exodus 3:13–15, John 1:1–3

**Clear answer**

In classical Christian belief, God was not created. God is understood as eternal and uncaused, the source of everything that begins to exist. If something created God, that creator would be more ultimate than God, so it would not be God in the Christian sense.

**Why this passage matters**

Psalm 90:2 describes God as existing before the mountains and from everlasting to everlasting. Christian philosophers distinguish God from things inside the universe: stars, people, and matter exist within time and depend on causes, while God is understood as the foundation of existence itself. This is not the same as saying that everything needs a creator. The usual argument is that things which begin to exist need an explanation; God, by definition, has no beginning.

**Key takeaway**

The Christian answer is that God is eternal, not another object that began to exist.

### FM061 | Why do Christians believe Jesus had to die?
*Medium · Big Question, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 10:45
**Supporting Scripture:** Romans 3:23–26, 1 Peter 3:18, 1 Corinthians 15:3–4

**Clear answer**

Christians believe Jesus had to die because sin damages humanity's relationship with God, and people cannot repair that damage by themselves. In Jesus, God takes the cost of reconciliation upon himself: Jesus gives his life, deals with sin, and defeats death through his resurrection. The cross is the centre of how Christianity understands salvation.

**Why this passage matters**

In Mark 10:45, Jesus describes his death as giving his life 'as a ransom for many'. The New Testament also describes the cross as sacrifice, forgiveness, reconciliation, victory over evil, and a rescue from slavery. These are different pictures of the same central claim: God acts through Jesus to bring people back to himself. Christians differ over exactly how these images fit together, but they do not believe God needed an unwilling victim. Jesus gives himself willingly, and the resurrection shows that death does not have the final word.

**Key takeaway**

For Christians, Jesus' death is how God acts to rescue and reconcile humanity.

### FM062 | What is the Trinity, and why is it not three gods?
*Medium · Big Question, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Matthew 28:19
**Supporting Scripture:** John 1:1–3, 2 Corinthians 13:14

**Clear answer**

The Trinity means there is one God who exists eternally as three distinct persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Christians do not mean three gods, and they do not mean one person pretending to be three different people.

**Why this passage matters**

The word 'Trinity' is not used in the Bible, but Christians developed it to describe the way the New Testament speaks about God. Jesus commands baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19). John 1 describes the Word as both with God and as God, while other passages speak of the Spirit as divine. The doctrine tries to hold these biblical claims together: one divine being, three persons, equal in divinity and distinct in relationship.

**Key takeaway**

The Trinity is one God, not three gods: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

### FM063 | Why do Christians believe Jesus rose from the dead?
*Medium · Big Question, Jesus · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 15:3–8
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 24:1–12, John 20:19–29

**Clear answer**

Christians believe Jesus rose because his first followers said they encountered him alive after his execution, and this claim appeared at the very beginning of the Christian movement. Paul preserves an early summary of the appearances, while the Gospels describe the discovery of the empty tomb and meetings with the risen Jesus.

**Why this passage matters**

In 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, Paul repeats a tradition he had received: Jesus died, was buried, was raised, and appeared to Peter, the Twelve, and many others. The letter was written within a few decades of Jesus' death, so resurrection belief was already central very early. The Gospels add accounts of the empty tomb and appearances. Historians can examine the age and reliability of these sources, but whether a resurrection miracle is the best explanation is a further historical and theological judgment. Christians accept it as the explanation that makes sense of the witnesses and the beginning of the church.

**Key takeaway**

The resurrection belief appears very early and is central to what Christians say happened to Jesus.

### FM064 | Can you believe in God and still have doubts?
*Medium · Big Question, Real Life · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Mark 9:14–29
**Supporting Scripture:** John 20:24–29, Psalm 13

**Clear answer**

Yes. Doubt does not automatically cancel faith. You can trust God while still having questions, feeling uncertain, or struggling to believe something fully.

**Why this passage matters**

## Science, History & the Bible

### FM080 | Did dinosaurs and humans live at the same time?
*Medium · Science, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Genesis 1:20–25
**Supporting Scripture:** Psalm 19:1–4

**Clear answer**

According to mainstream scientific evidence, humans did not live alongside non-avian dinosaurs. Those dinosaurs died out about 66 million years before modern humans appeared. Birds are living descendants of theropod dinosaurs, so in that scientific sense dinosaurs still live among us.

**Why this passage matters**

Genesis 1 tells the creation story in theological language about God as creator and the world as ordered and good. It does not give a scientific timeline of dinosaur fossils or human evolution. Fossils, rock layers, and other evidence place the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs around 66 million years ago; modern humans appeared much later. Christians differ on how to interpret the days in Genesis, but the scientific evidence for the timing is strong.

**Key takeaway**

Non-avian dinosaurs lived millions of years before humans; birds are the dinosaur lineage that survives today.

### FM081 | Does the Bible say the Earth is flat?
*Medium · Science, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Isaiah 40:22
**Supporting Scripture:** Job 26:7, Psalm 104:1–5

**Clear answer**

No. The Bible does not teach that the earth is a flat disc. It uses poetry, everyday descriptions, and ancient imagery, but those expressions should not be mistaken for a scientific model of the planet.

**Why this passage matters**

Isaiah 40:22 says God sits above the 'circle of the earth'. The Hebrew word describes a circle or horizon and does not function as a technical statement about the earth's geometry. Other passages speak poetically about the foundations or pillars of the earth. Their purpose is to describe God's power and creation using familiar imagery, not to map the planet. The shape of the earth is established by extensive scientific observation, and it is roughly spherical.

**Key takeaway**

Biblical poetry is not a flat-earth diagram; the earth is approximately spherical.

### FM082 | How did the Bible come together, and who chose the books?
*Medium · History, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** Luke 1:1–4
**Supporting Scripture:** 2 Timothy 3:14–17, 2 Peter 3:15–16

**Clear answer**

The Bible was not assembled in one meeting where a group of leaders invented its books. Its writings were composed over many centuries, copied, shared, read in worship, and gradually recognized as authoritative by Jewish and Christian communities. The New Testament's 27-book collection became widely settled in the early church over several centuries.

**Why this passage matters**

Luke 1:1–4 shows that a Gospel writer used earlier accounts and investigated the events carefully. For the New Testament, churches valued writings connected to apostles, consistent with the faith they had received, and widely used across Christian communities. Athanasius listed the same 27 books in 367 CE, and regional councils later confirmed that list. Christians differ over some Old Testament books, especially between Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox Bibles. Councils recognized and confirmed a collection; they did not simply make the books authoritative by voting.

**Key takeaway**

The biblical collection took shape over time through the use and recognition of its writings.

### FM085 | What evidence is there that Jesus was a real historical person?
*Medium · History, Curious · 3 min*

**Primary Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 15:3–8
**Supporting Scripture:** Luke 1:1–4, Acts 2:22–24

**Clear answer**

Several sources support Jesus' existence. The Gospels and Paul's letters are early Christian writings, and non-Christian writers such as the Roman historian Tacitus and the Jewish historian Josephus also refer to Jesus or the movement that formed around him. Historians broadly agree that Jesus was a first-century Jewish teacher who was crucified under Pontius Pilate.

**Why this passage matters**

Tacitus, writing in the early second century, says Christus was executed under Pontius Pilate during the reign of Tiberius. Josephus refers to James as the brother of Jesus who was called Christ; a separate, longer passage about Jesus appears to have been altered by later Christian copyists. These sources are not equally detailed, and they do not prove Christian claims such as the resurrection or Jesus' divinity. They do make the claim that Jesus never existed an extremely weak position among historians.

**Key takeaway**

Historians broadly agree Jesus existed and was crucified; claims about his divine identity and resurrection involve further historical and theological questions.

The Gospels sometimes describe the same event with different details because each writer selected and arranged material for a particular purpose. **Key takeaway**

The Gospel writers tell the story with their own selection and emphasis, so compare what each account actually says.

The snake bite becomes a sign in the story, but it is not a promise of immunity from danger.

### FM126 | Why did Jesus tell Peter to find a coin inside a fish's mouth?

Jesus says he is free from the tax, yet provides the money to avoid an unnecessary dispute.

### FM130 | Why did Jesus curse a fig tree?

### FM132 | Why did Peter start sinking when he walked on water?

**Supporting Scripture:** Mark 6:45–52, Matthew 8:23–27

Peter starts sinking when he becomes afraid of the wind and waves and stops focusing on Jesus. He calls for help, and Jesus immediately reaches out and catches him. The story shows Peter's courage and weakness at the same time.
